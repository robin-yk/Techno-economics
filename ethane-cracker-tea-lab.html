Known limitations

1. Table S6 belongs to the LCA, not the TEA.

SI Note 2 (TEA) contains no inventory table; Table S6 sits in SI Note 3 and is described as the inventory for the life-cycle assessment. Using it as the OPEX basis is this tool's reconstruction, and the Validation tab shows where that bridge fails.

2. Exact utility prices are referenced but not printed.

Assumption 14 cites Seider et al. for utility costs. Numerical LP/HP steam, refrigeration, electricity, and water prices need the original Aspen/Seider basis. The Validation tab quantifies how far the screening defaults are off.

3. TBMC is not published.

Defaults are back-calculated from the TCI reported in Section 2.5 assuming TCI ≈ TOC. That equivalence is an assumption; TCI may include working capital that TOC does not.

4. CFP replacement mass depends on unpublished geometry.

Assumption 7 fixes the replacement interval and assumption 6 fixes tube count and diameter, but the reactor volume still depends on the inlet flow and residence time, which the SI does not report.

5. Efficiency is already embedded in modeled utilities.

The 60% furnace and 95% Joule assumptions should not multiply the full Table S6 electricity inventory again. The separate heat-duty calculator avoids double counting.

6. Capacity is shared for route comparison.

Assumption 1 gives 600,000–620,000 t/y and SI §3.1 gives ~77 t/h. The default 610,000 t/y is the range midpoint and can be changed.

7. COO and MSP are used interchangeably in the source.

Assumption 5 speaks of a minimum selling price while assumption 13 defines COO, and only Eq. S8 is given. Comparisons on the Validation tab therefore put Eq. S8 output against the published product cost.

8. No uncertainty interval was reported.

The sensitivity and tornado charts vary inputs deterministically. They do not provide a statistical confidence interval or an Aspen estimate class.

The one number this tool produces

Everything funnels into cost of ethylene (COO) — the all-in dollars it takes to make one tonne of C2H4. Build it in four steps:

1

What the equipment costs to buy and install

TOC = 2.5 × TBMC

TBMC (total bare-module cost) is the price of the bare equipment — the columns, compressors and heat exchangers — as Aspen quotes them. Multiplying by 2.5 gives TOC (total overnight cost): what it costs to actually have a working plant standing there, including piping, instruments, civil work and engineering. “Overnight” means the number ignores interest during construction, as if the plant appeared overnight.

2

Spreading that capital over the plant's life

CAPEX = TOC × CCF

You do not pay TOC every year. The CCF (capital charge factor) of 0.1 converts the one-time build cost into an equivalent annual payment — roughly “10% of the build cost per year” — covering repayment, interest and return to investors. So CAPEX here means the annualized capital charge, not the upfront spend.

3

What it costs to run for a year

OPEX = feed + utilities + treatment + CFP − coproduct credits

OPEX (operating expenditure) is everything consumed per year: ethane feed, steam, refrigeration, electricity, water, TEG, wastewater treatment and CFP replacement. Then subtract coproduct credits — the plant also makes LPG and fuel gas, and selling those offsets the bill. CO&M adds maintenance (2.5% of TOC), insurance (5% of TOC) and $5 M/y of labor.

4

Divide by how much product you made

COO = (CAPEX + OPEX + CO&M) / mC2H4

Total annual dollars ÷ tonnes of ethylene per year = dollars per tonne. That is the headline figure on the dashboard and the thing compared against the manuscript.

How to read each chart

Cost stack by route

A horizontal bar per route, split into four cost blocks. The vertical line is zero. Costs grow to the right; the green coproduct credit bar grows to the left, on the same dollar scale — so if the green bar looks a tenth as long as the coloured stack, the credit really is about a tenth of the cost. The small black caret marks the net result: stack length minus credit length. Hover any block for its exact value.

Price sensitivity

Four lines showing how each route's cost of ethylene rises as ethane gets more expensive. Each route has its own colour and its own dash pattern and marker shape, so the lines stay distinguishable in greyscale. The dashed vertical line is your current ethane price; the markers sit where each route lands at that price. The electricity slider moves all four lines up or down together.

Tornado

Answers “which input actually matters?” Each row varies one input up and down by the chosen percentage while holding everything else fixed, and draws how far the cost of ethylene moves. Rows are sorted longest-first, so the top row is your biggest risk. The light blue half is the low end of that input, the dark blue half is the high end. The right-hand figure is the half-width in $/t.

Validation table

Each cell shows the calculated value, the published value beneath it, and the percentage difference. Green is within 10% of the manuscript, amber within 25%, red beyond that. Where the manuscript gives a range rather than one number, anything landing inside the range reads as 0.0%.
